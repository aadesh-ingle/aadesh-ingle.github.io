Evals Are the Operating System

Aadesh Ingle
April 12, 2026
4 min read

One of our customers runs a freight platform where an AI agent negotiates loads over email with real brokers. Real counterparties, real rates, real money. When the model gets a negotiation wrong, it's not a demo bug. It's margin leaking out of the business, and a broker on the other side quietly losing trust in the product.

For a while, we judged quality the way most teams do: read some threads, nod, ship. Vibe-checks. And honestly, vibe-checks work fine right up until they don't. Ours stopped working when traffic grew past what anyone could read. Somewhere in those threads, the agent was being more eager to close than it should have been, agreeing to things it had no business agreeing to. We caught it late, from the threads themselves. The dollar impact we could only estimate from the affected conversations; we never had a clean before/after, and I won't pretend we did.

That was uncomfortable. What made it urgent was a model migration.

The migration that forced the issue

We needed to move from GPT-4o to GPT-4.1. Anyone who has done a model swap in production knows the fear: the new model is better on average and different in the specifics, and "different in the specifics" is where regressions live. Nobody reads ten thousand email threads to find them. Without measurement, the first person to catch a regression is a customer.

I pushed for building a proper eval suite before the migration, and I'll be honest: it felt like it was slowing us down. It was two steps back on a roadmap that had plenty of forward steps waiting. The argument that won was simple. We were about to change the engine of the product, and we had no instruments on the dashboard.

The suite ended up at 87 test functions and 19 golden cases pulled from real negotiations. The design decision I'd defend most is the split:

  • Hard assertions for anything with a right answer. Rate boundaries, compliance rules, things the agent must never offer. No model judges these. Code does.
  • LLM-as-judge only for tone. Is the reply professional? Does it read like a negotiator and not a chatbot? And before trusting the judge, we validated it against human ratings on the same samples. If the judge can't agree with a human, it doesn't get a vote.

I see teams do this the other way around: an LLM judge scoring everything, including the parts that have exact answers. That's how you end up with a flaky eval suite nobody trusts, which is worse than no suite, because it gives you confidence instead of information.

Get the risky logic out of the prompt

The evals exposed a second problem. Some negotiation behavior lived in the prompt, which means it lived at the model's discretion. Discretion is exactly what you don't want around money.

So we moved the negotiation policy (what can be offered, when, within what bounds) out of the prompt and into server-side code. The model drafts the language; the policy decides the numbers. After that change, a whole class of failure became impossible rather than unlikely. A prompt can drift with a model version. An if statement doesn't.

That snippet is illustrative, not our code. But the shape is exactly right: boring, deterministic, testable. The most valuable line of "AI engineering" I wrote that quarter was not a prompt.

What the suite actually bought us

We ran the GPT-4o to 4.1 migration through the evals. They caught two real regressions, behaviors that had quietly changed and would have reached brokers otherwise. We fixed both before cutover. Customers never saw them. As far as the business was concerned, the engine swap was a non-event, which is the highest compliment a migration can get.

That eval loop is what lets the system run at the scale it runs today, 80,000+ emails and 10,000+ loads across five production tenants, without anyone reading threads all day hoping to spot trouble.

The framing I've landed on: evals aren't a QA step you bolt on at the end. They're the operating system of an AI product once real users arrive. Every other improvement runs on top of them, because without them you can't tell whether you improved anything at all. You shipped a change and the vibes were good. That's not engineering.

If you're running an LLM feature in production and a model migration scares you, that fear is data. It's telling you that you don't have instruments. Build them before the swap, not after the incident.
