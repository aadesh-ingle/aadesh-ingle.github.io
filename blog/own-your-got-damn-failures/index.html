Own the Failure Before It Becomes a Story

After watching a friend write carefully about a breakup, I started treating personal failures more like postmortems: record what happened before memory makes my role smaller.

March 21, 20253 min readWriting

A close friend kept a notebook open while going through a breakup. He used it to name what had happened and where he had contributed to it. The notes were private, painful, and written without an audience.

I recognized the opposite instinct in myself. When something hurts, I start editing too soon. I shrink my part, smooth the sequence into something inevitable, and reach for a lesson that makes the episode sound useful. Time helps with the pain, but it also gives memory more room to revise the record.

Write before memory edits#

His questions were plain: What did I ignore? What did I assume would fix itself? Where did I confuse wanting something with taking care of it? Writing down the answers removed the comfort of being only the person something happened to.

The same editing happens after failed work, bad decisions, and arguments I mishandled. If I wait, I remember my intentions more clearly than my actions. The note has to be written while the sequence is still available and before I can flatter myself.

Engineering postmortems offer a structure I can borrow. They preserve the timeline, assumptions, actions, effects, and what people understood at the time. A private record can do the same without turning into self-punishment.

What this post is in conversation with

  • Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture: Separates detailed accountability from punishment, the same distinction I want a private failure record to preserve.
  • Postmortem Culture: Frames a postmortem by the learning and concrete changes it produces rather than by the severity of the incident.

Own the part that is yours#

Accountability becomes useless when it expands into accepting every accusation. The narrower task is to find the part that was mine and describe it without softening it.

That share may be small or it may explain most of what happened. Naming it gives me something specific to change when a similar situation appears again.

I am suspicious now of lessons that leave me unchanged. “Everything happens for a reason” is comforting because it asks nothing. “I avoided a difficult conversation for three weeks” asks something. It gives me a place to intervene next time.

The rule I want to keep#

When something hurts, I want to delay the lesson and write the record first: what happened, what I wanted to be true, what I knew but did not say, and what I missed.

The last line is the practical one: what would I do if the same situation appeared again? If the note cannot answer that, I have probably turned the experience into a story too soon.
