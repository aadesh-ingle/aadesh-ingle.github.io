The Model Is the Easy Part

Aadesh Ingle
June 2, 2026
3 min read

Every AI product I've worked on has had the same shape. The model call — the part everyone wants to talk about — is a few hundred lines. Everything around it is the product: permissions, guardrails, evals, observability, retries, the decision about how much of the workflow the model should even be allowed to touch. The model is the easy part. It arrives pre-trained. The rest you have to earn.

I keep meeting teams who get this backwards, and I understand why. The model is where the magic visibly lives. A founder sees a demo, the demo is astonishing, and the natural conclusion is that the work is mostly done. But a demo only has to work once, in front of a friendly audience, on input chosen by the person demoing. A product has to work on Tuesday afternoon, on the worst input of the month, while nobody is watching. The distance between those two is not closed by a better model. It's closed by engineering that has nothing to do with intelligence.

A few convictions I've collected from shipping these systems, offered with the usual caveat that they were learned by getting things wrong first.

Start from the user's day, not the capability. The teams I've watched lose the most velocity started with a capability — an agent, a copilot, whatever the season's word is — and went looking for somewhere to put it. The question that actually opens a project is embarrassingly old-fashioned: walk me through the workflow, show me where the friction is, show me what gets dropped or done badly because a human is out of time. Sometimes the answer is an LLM. Sometimes it's a queue and a form. Caring about the answer more than the technology is most of the job.

Decide what the model is not allowed to decide. The most important architectural call in any system I've shipped was drawing the line between what the model proposes and what the code enforces. Anything involving money, compliance, or an irreversible action goes on the code side of the line. A prompt is a suggestion to a statistical process; it can drift with the next model version. Behavior that must never happen should be impossible, not unlikely — and "impossible" is spelled with an if statement, not with capital letters in the system prompt.

Don't fight the model longer than the evidence supports. You can't out-prompt a wrong architecture — I've tried, and I wrote up how that went on a voice product. There's a version of persistence that's really just attachment to your first design. When production keeps producing the same class of failure and your fixes keep moving the needle less each time, the system is telling you something. Low ego is operationally useful here: being comfortable deleting your own work is a competitive advantage in a field where the first version is usually wrong in some way you couldn't have predicted.

The outer loop is the durable advantage. Model quality is a rented advantage — whatever you have, your competitor can call the same API next quarter. What compounds is the loop around it: evals that tell you whether a change helped, observability that turns a vague complaint into a traceable failure, retrieval and context that improve with every correction, feedback from real usage flowing back into the system. Two teams with identical models will diverge completely on the strength of their outer loops. I've never seen prompt cleverness compound. I've watched eval suites do it.

None of this is a complaint about the models, which are remarkable and getting more so. It's an argument about where the work is. When the next astonishing demo crosses your feed, the question that separates the products from the demos is unchanged: what happens around the model when it's wrong? Because in production, on a long enough timeline, it will be — and the system you built around it is the difference between an incident and a non-event.

That system is the job. The model is the easy part.
