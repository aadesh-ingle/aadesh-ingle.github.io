Golden Cases Come From Funerals

Aadesh IngleJUN 07 · 2026
Try it yourselfIncident to regression workbench ↓

People ask where our eval test cases come from. They expect a methodology, maybe a framework with a name. The honest answer is less impressive and more useful: every golden case in our suites is the gravestone of something that hurt us once.

The agent that agreed to a rate it shouldn't have? There's a golden case with that exact thread shape now. The tool call that misfired on a particular phrasing? That phrasing is in the suite, verbatim. The extraction that silently swapped two cities with similar names? Those two cities live in the test set forever, a tiny monument to an afternoon nobody enjoyed.

This sounds like an admission of failure, and it is. It's also the only test-creation methodology I've seen survive contact with production.

Why invented cases underperform

When you write test cases from imagination, you test the failures you can imagine. But the failures you can imagine are, almost by definition, the ones you already defended against while building. The model rarely breaks where you pointed your worry. It breaks where reality is shaped differently than your mental model of it, and the only reliable supplier of that information is reality itself.

So the discipline is less "design a comprehensive test suite" and more "never waste a funeral." Every production incident, every user complaint that traces to a real failure, every correction a reviewer makes in the loop: each one is a labeled example of the system being wrong, donated by the world, at a price you've already paid. Turning it into a golden case is how you stop paying twice.

Inspect the fixture’s provenance

The incident classes come from the article. Candidate behaviors and symbolic assertions are teaching fixtures, not reports of production test results. The rate fixture uses invented units and an inclusive 1800–2200 boundary: the original failure agrees above the ceiling, the regression agrees below the floor, and the fix rejects without agreeing. In a real case, retain the exact failing input, relevant retrieved context, tool arguments, and timestamps where needed; redact private details without removing the failure shape.

Attach a stable name, incident date, owner, and why the case exists. Prefer deterministic assertions for tool choice, canonical IDs, and policy boundaries; use a judge only when the behavior actually requires judgment.

The mechanics, such as they are

Our habit is simple enough to fit in a paragraph. When a failure is diagnosed, the fix isn't done until the case that exposed it is in the suite. Not a paraphrase of it, the actual input, anonymized where needed, with an assertion that captures what "correct" would have been. The pull request that fixes the bug and the pull request that immortalizes it are the same pull request.

Two details matter more than they look:

  1. Keep the case even after the architecture changes. The failure was a fact about your problem domain, not about your implementation. New systems find old ways to be wrong with remarkable consistency.
  2. Record why it's there. A one-line comment with the date and the incident. A suite full of unexplained cases becomes folklore, and folklore gets deleted by the next person cleaning up.

One more detail I would add now: keep the trace shape, not only the text. If the failure involved retrieval, keep the retrieved context. If it involved a tool, keep the tool arguments. If it involved timing, keep the timestamps. A golden case that saves only the final user message is often a unit test for the wrong layer.

Over time something pleasant happens: the suite becomes a history of every way the system has ever embarrassed you, which is exactly the thing a new model version needs to be tested against. When we migrate models, we are literally asking: does the new engine repeat any of our old funerals? It's a question imagination could never have written.

The suites I trust grew this way, incident by incident, and the suites I distrust were authored in a week by someone thorough. Thoroughness is a fine quality. It's just no substitute for grief.
