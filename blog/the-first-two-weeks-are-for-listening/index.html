The First Two Weeks Are for Listening

Aadesh IngleMAY 17 · 2026

The first thing I want from a new engagement isn't access to the codebase. It's an hour with whoever actually does the work. The ops person with eleven browser tabs. The coordinator who knows which customers call instead of emailing. The reviewer who can spot a bad record at a glance but can't fully explain how. Walk me through yesterday. Not the process diagram. Yesterday, the real one, with the workaround you're slightly embarrassed about.

I've learned to protect this phase stubbornly, because every expensive failure I've seen in client AI work was already inevitable before the first line of code. Not wrong models. Wrong problems. Somebody fell in love with a capability, went looking for a place to put it, and found one. The system then worked exactly as designed and changed nothing that mattered, which is the cruelest kind of failure, because there's no bug to fix.

Set the solution aside

The founder conversation that starts these engagements usually arrives with a solution attached: "we want an agent for X." The single most useful thing I do, more useful honestly than anything I build later, is to gently set the solution aside and ask about the day.

  • Where does work pile up?
  • What gets dropped when things are busy?
  • What does someone retype from one screen into another?
  • What do your best people do that your newest people can't?

Friction hides in those answers, and friction is where the value is. The day always wins the argument with the org chart's theory of the day.

What the listening buys

Two weeks of this feels slow from the outside. Founders are paying, and they want to see velocity. But about half the time, the right first build is not the thing that was asked for. Sometimes the workflow's worst bottleneck needs a queue and a form, not a model, and shipping that first earns more trust than any demo. Sometimes the AI opportunity is real but sits one step earlier in the pipeline than anyone guessed, because the step everyone complains about is downstream of the step that actually causes it.

You cannot see this from a requirements document. The document records what people believe about their work; the walkthrough shows you what their work is. The distance between those two is where projects die before the code starts.

There's a humility to this that the AI moment makes hard. We're carrying the most impressive technology of our careers, and the discipline is to walk in pretending, for two weeks, that we aren't carrying anything. To be more curious about their Tuesday than about our toolkit. The engineers I've watched do this well treat the domain experts as the geniuses in the room, because about their own workflow, they are.

The pattern has held across every deployment I've shipped: the quality of the listening predicts the fate of the system better than the quality of the engineering. Which is an uncomfortable thing for an engineer to type. Years spent sharpening the building, and the deciding factor turns out to be sharpened somewhere else. The consolation is that listening, it turns out, is also a craft. The first two weeks are for practicing it.
