Trading Voice Quality for Reliability

Aadesh IngleFEB 08 · 2026

One of our customers runs a recruitment product where candidates talk to an AI by voice. The whole pitch is the conversation. It should feel like talking to a person, not navigating a phone tree. And users were getting frustrated and dropping out. Not because the voice sounded robotic, but because mid-conversation the agent kept failing to actually do things: pull up the right information, trigger the right action on screen. A natural-sounding agent that doesn't do what you asked is worse than a clunky one that does.

The founder was worried, and he was right to be. For a product whose entire value is trust in a conversation, this was the one thing that couldn't be broken.

Tracing it to the architecture

We had built on OpenAI's Realtime API. Speech in, speech out, one model handling everything. It's the obvious choice for a voice product and the demos are genuinely magical. But when I traced the user complaints, they kept landing in the same place: tool calls firing unreliably. The conversation flowed beautifully and the actions misfired. The docs and the community threads confirmed we weren't alone. At the time, others were hitting the same wall with tool calling on the Realtime preview.

My first instinct was the usual one. Tune the prompt, reword the tool descriptions, try again. We did some of that. It moved the needle a little and then stopped moving it. At some point you have to admit you're swimming upstream against the model, and the current is stronger than you are.

Reframing it as a product decision

The conversation that mattered wasn't technical. It was reframing the choice for the founder: our users don't need the most natural voice possible. They need the right thing to happen, reliably, every time they ask. Naturalness is what makes the demo. Reliability is what makes the product.

Once it was framed that way, the architecture decision made itself. We researched the alternatives (Pipecat, LiveKit) and spent about a month building a POC of the unfashionable thing: a stitched pipeline. Speech-to-text, then a text LLM with proper tool calling, then text-to-speech. Three hops instead of one. More latency, more moving parts, less magic.

Then we did the part I'd insist on in any team I work with: we decided with evals, not opinions. We built a test set of statements a candidate might say and measured tool-trigger reliability on the pipeline. Around 40 of 50 statements triggered the right tool in our first baseline pass. To be clear, that was our own rough harness, not a published benchmark. More useful than the number was the failure analysis. Most misses traced back to overlapping tool descriptions, which was fixable. The Realtime misses had no pattern we could act on. A failure you can explain is a failure you can fix.

The cost was real: responses landed at roughly 1.2 seconds, noticeably slower than the Realtime path. We took the tradeoff with our eyes open, because for this workflow a beat of silence was survivable and a wrong action was not.

The important detail is that the 1.2 seconds became inspectable. In the fused path, "it felt slow" and "it chose the wrong action" were hard to separate. In the stitched path, each stage had a receipt. If STT was slow, we saw it. If the LLM spent the budget choosing a tool, we saw it. If TTS blocked playback, we saw it. That is the hidden value of an uglier architecture: it gives you places to put instruments.

What happened after

We shipped it. The reliability complaints stopped. And that "temporary" stitched architecture stayed the core of the product for about a year and a half, long past the point where anyone called it a compromise.

Two things I carry from this one.

First: when production evidence contradicts your architecture, the cheapest time to change is now. We could have spent six more months patching prompts on a foundation that didn't fit the workflow, and every one of those months would have made the rewrite more expensive.

Second: the tradeoffs that feel like engineering decisions are usually product decisions wearing a disguise. Latency versus reliability wasn't mine to decide by preference. It fell out of asking what the user actually came to do.

Would I build it the same way today? Probably not. Realtime voice models have improved a lot since then, and I'd re-run the same evals before assuming anything. That's rather the point. The architecture was never the conviction. The eval loop was.
