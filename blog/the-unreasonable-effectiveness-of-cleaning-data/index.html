The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Cleaning Data

Aadesh IngleAPR 14 · 2024
Try it yourselfEntity resolution bench ↓

A confession about a quarter of my working life: while the entire industry was talking about LLMs, I spent it on transporter records. Missing identifiers. Duplicates. Fuzzy matching. The least glamorous work in our logistics customer's stack, and, I'd argue now, some of the highest-leverage work I've done for them.

The setup: the customer's demand data depended on knowing the status of transporters. Who's active, who's verified, who's actually the same company entered three times by three different people. The records were polluted in every way operational data gets polluted. Identifiers missing. Names spelled four ways. The same transporter alive in the system as four entities, each carrying a fragment of the truth. And nothing downstream announces this. Dashboards render. Reports generate. Every number is wrong by an amount nobody can name.

That's the thing about dirty reference data. It doesn't fail loudly. It taxes everything silently. Every analysis, every automation, every decision built on those records inherited the pollution. Garbage in, garbage out is the cliché; the operational reality is worse, because the garbage is invisible by the time it's out.

The unfashionable work

The work itself was satisfying in a way that's hard to explain to anyone chasing the model of the week.

  • Thorough EDA before touching anything. You cannot fix data you haven't actually looked at, and the distributions surprised me more than once.
  • Fuzzy matching to resolve name variants, with thresholds tuned by reading the borderline cases myself rather than picking a similarity score that felt right.
  • Duplicate analysis to collapse the multi-entity transporters back into single records.
  • An XGBoost-ensemble classifier to predict transporter status where ground truth was missing. Useful, but I want to be precise about its place: the model existed to serve the cleanup. The cleanup was the product.

I never published an accuracy headline for this work and I won't invent one here. The honest outcome was qualitative: downstream systems could finally trust the transporter table.

Value that compounds

What struck me was how the value spread. Clean reference data isn't a feature; it's a multiplier on every feature that touches it. The demand pipeline got more trustworthy without a single change to the demand pipeline. That's a strange and wonderful property: fixing things you didn't touch.

I think about this often now that every conversation starts with models. There's a hierarchy of leverage in data systems, and it runs the opposite direction from the hype. The model is replaceable. The pipeline is durable. The data quality underneath both is foundational. Teams reach for the most sophisticated layer first because that's where the excitement is. But sophistication built on polluted records is precision about fiction.

If your sights are set on doing impressive things with AI this year, ask the unfashionable question first: would you bet money on your reference data being right? If the answer makes you wince, you already know where the leverage is. It's just not where the fun is. Until you've done it once, and watched every downstream system stop arguing with the table beneath it.
