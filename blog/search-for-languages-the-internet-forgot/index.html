Search for Languages the Internet Forgot

Aadesh IngleJUL 09 · 2022
Try it yourselfRetrieval ranking laboratory ↓

There's a version of search research where everything works. You take a dense retriever, train it on millions of English query-document pairs, and watch it embarrass BM25 on the standard benchmarks. Most of the papers I read during my Master's were set in that world: abundant data, mature tooling, benchmarks polished smooth by years of attention.

My problem wasn't set in that world. I worked on dense retrieval for low-resource languages: languages where the training pairs don't exist, the web corpus is thin, and the tooling assumes you'll be fine because multilingual models exist now. You will not automatically be fine. "Supported" is not the same thing as "served well."

The gap nobody benchmarks

The distance between "multilingual model supports your language" and "retrieval actually works in your language" turned out to be the whole thesis. A multilingual encoder has seen some text in the language. That is not the same as having learned what makes two pieces of text about the same thing in that language, which is the actual job of a retriever. Tokenization fragments words the model rarely saw. Embedding space for the language is sparser and lumpier than for English. Queries and documents that should be neighbors end up merely in the same neighborhood, and at retrieval time, "same neighborhood" is the difference between rank 1 and rank 40.

Most of my time went where research time actually goes: not the model, the data. Building usable training and evaluation sets when no benchmark hands them to you. Deciding what counts as a relevant document when annotation budgets are near zero. Being suspicious of my own evaluation set, because with small data a handful of mislabeled pairs can swing your metrics enough to tell you a comfortable lie.

That was the part I underestimated before starting. Low-resource retrieval is not only a modeling problem with less data. It is an evidence problem. You spend as much time asking "how would we know this worked?" as "how do we make it work?"

The newer multilingual retrieval benchmarks make this explicit. MIRACL's value is not only that it covers 18 languages; it is that native speakers produced relevance judgments at scale. Mr. TyDi's useful surprise was that multilingual dense retrieval did not automatically beat BM25 everywhere, but hybrid signals helped. That is the lesson I wish I had started with: in low-resource retrieval, the benchmark construction is part of the system, not a side quest.

Five invented English glosses with fixed, illustrative lexical and semantic scores on the same 0–1 scale. The blend is a teaching model, not BM25 or a trained retriever. Real hybrid systems must reconcile score scales. Labels change metric judgments without changing ranking scores. This is one query’s reciprocal rank, not mean reciprocal rank; the article’s 81% accuracy and 57.83% MRR are separate historical measurements.

Where it landed

81% retrieval accuracy and an MRR of 57.83% on our evaluation. I'll resist the urge to dress those up. Against what English-language systems post on mature benchmarks, they're modest. Against the baselines available for the setting, they were real progress. And more importantly, the failure analysis told us where the remaining errors lived, which is worth more than the score itself.

What stays with me from this work isn't the architecture. It's a habit: the quality of retrieval is decided by the quality of what you feed it and how you measure it, long before it's decided by the model. The fancy encoder gets the citation. The dataset construction does the work.

The operating rule I took from it: never confuse language coverage with language quality. A model can accept text in a language and still fail the people who need to search, retrieve, and build with it.

And a quieter thought, the reason I picked the problem at all. The next generation of language technology is being built on whatever text the internet happens to contain. For a few hundred languages, that's an ocean. For thousands of others, it's a puddle, and the people who speak them will inherit tools that work a little worse, every day, in a thousand small ways. Somebody has to care about the puddles.
